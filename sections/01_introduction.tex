\section{INTRODUCTION}
\label{sec:intro}

In practical deployment scenarios, due to acquisition costs, equipment setup constraints, and limited camera trajectories, it is often difficult to obtain multi-view images with sufficient coverage. Compared with dense-view capture, sparse inputs provide only limited information about visible regions. As a result, 3D reconstruction methods tend to overfit local observations from sparse inputs, making it difficult to maintain global geometric consistency~\cite{ref2}. In such an underconstrained setting, sparse observations introduce greater geometric ambiguity, making occluded regions, textureless areas, and view boundaries more prone to missing geometry, structural distortions, and degraded appearance reconstruction quality~\cite{ref3}. Effectively estimating and completing the geometry and appearance of unseen regions, while establishing a stable initial geometric scaffold and reducing noise propagation from unreliable supervision, remains a critical challenge in sparse-view 3D reconstruction.

To improve reconstruction efficiency, Kerbl et al.~\cite{ref10} introduced 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), an explicit scene representation and rendering framework. This method represents scene geometry and appearance with differentiable 3D Gaussian ellipsoids, whose position, covariance, color, and opacity are jointly optimized. By adopting efficient tiled rasterization, 3DGS enables rapid training and real-time rendering~\cite{ref11}. Compared to implicit neural representation methods, the explicit representation and rasterization strategy of 3DGS offer significant advantages in efficiency.

However, under sparse-view conditions, traditional Structure-from-Motion (SfM)~\cite{ref24} often recovers only sparse and incomplete point clouds. Since the 3DGS pipeline typically uses SfM-calibrated camera parameters and sparse point clouds as initialization inputs, insufficient initialization directly affects the optimization stability and geometric convergence of Gaussian primitives~\cite{ref25}. The lack of cross-view constraints can further lead to geometric degradation, floaters, and pseudo-geometry~\cite{ref26}. During optimization, effective supervision primarily comes from a few visible regions~\cite{ref37}, and the model tends to overfit the sparse training observations, degrading novel-view rendering quality and leading to artifacts.

Building upon 3DGS, existing sparse-view methods have explored depth regularization, representation improvements, and generative priors~\cite{ref12,ref13,ref14,ref15,ref16}. Most existing 3DGS-based sparse reconstruction methods rely on depth information as supervision and typically employ predefined sampling strategies to initialize 3D Gaussians. While these approaches improve reconstruction quality to some extent, they still struggle to fully capture intricate details and maintain geometric consistency under sparse observations.

To address this issue, we present SynGS, a 3D Gaussian Splatting method guided by geometric and depth priors for sparse-view 3D reconstruction. SynGS comprises three stages: \textbf{Visual Hull Initialization}, \textbf{VGGT / Depth-Guided Densification}, and \textbf{Dynamic Visibility Regularization}. The first two stages strengthen Gaussian initialization with explicit geometric constraints and depth-guided densification, while Dynamic Visibility Regularization alleviates overfitting during optimization. The main contributions are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Visual Hull Initialization.} We use multi-view silhouettes to construct a visual hull and generate a conservative yet stable point cloud, which serves as the geometric prior for Gaussian initialization and constrains the overall scene structure under sparse-view settings.
\item \textbf{VGGT / Depth-Guided Densification.} We introduce a depth-guided densification pipeline that uses cross-view consistent depth predictions to identify reliable regions and back-project supplementary points, providing a more complete geometric initialization for 3DGS.
\item \textbf{Dynamic Visibility Regularization.} We adopt Dynamic Visibility Regularization during 3DGS training to redistribute optimization gradients by randomly masking a subset of Gaussian primitives, alleviating overfitting to foreground regions and allowing distant and weakly visible regions to receive more sufficient gradient updates.
\end{itemize}
